# Towards an Emotionally Expressive Audio Omni-Model: An Open Annotation, Corpus, and Voice-Acting Pipeline

**A LAION technical white paper**

---

## Abstract

The goal of this program is to train an *omni-model* that not only understands and produces text, but also understands audio and **produces** audio — specifically expressive **voice acting** and TTS that spans the full range of human emotion, age groups, accents, and speaking styles, rather than the narrow, "call-center" register that dominates current systems. Reaching that goal requires three things in sequence: (1) high-quality annotation and filtration tooling, which we already have in the form of CLAP/Voice-CLAP retrieval models, the *Empathic Insight Voice Plus* emotion scorer, the *BUD-E Whisper* captioning family, the *MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking* audio-reasoning foundation model, and our *EmoNet* / *VoiceNet* / *vocal-burst* taxonomies; (2) a **paralinguistic inline-annotation ASR model** that transcribes not only *what* is said but *how* it is said — vocal bursts, whispering, shouting, sarcasm, emotional shifts mid-sentence; and (3) two complementary corpora — **LAION Voice**, a very large in-the-wild speech corpus annotated at scale, and **LAION Voice Acting**, a fully permissively-licensed synthetic corpus that is emotionally denser and richer in instruction-tuning structure than anything we can mine from the wild.

This document lays out the asset base, the model inventory, and the full construction plan for both corpora and the annotation model that ties them together.

---

## 1. Vision and Motivation

Today's open speech models are competent transcribers and adequate readers of neutral text. They are poor *actors*. They cannot reliably whisper a secret in an elevator, deliver a church sermon, rant, plead on a deathbed, or shift from tenderness to fury inside a single utterance — and they cannot be *instructed* to do so in fine-grained, controllable terms. The bottleneck is data: there is no large, openly-licensed corpus that pairs speech with fine-grained descriptions of emotion, talking style, paralinguistics, and *magnitude*, and no corpus that contains the controlled contrasts (same speaker, same text, different emotion) needed to teach instruction-following and voice cloning.

Our strategy is to build that data in two layers:

- **LAION Voice** — breadth and naturalism. A massive in-the-wild corpus (podcasts and Creative-Commons audio) annotated with our paralinguistic inline-ASR model plus emotion/quality scorers. This captures the full, messy, creative distribution of real human speech.
- **LAION Voice Acting** — depth and control. A synthetic corpus generated by modern open-weight voice-acting and voice-cloning models, then aggressively quality-filtered and ranked. Its distribution is narrower than the wild, but it is emotionally denser, and — crucially — it contains *structure that does not exist in the wild*: multiple magnitude variants of the same performance, and same-speaker emotion contrasts with known ground truth.

On licensing: **LAION Voice Acting** is generated end-to-end from permissively-licensed or synthetic voice identities and can be released directly. **LAION Voice** follows the LAION-5B model: for the non-permissive portion we release source URLs plus timestamps and annotations, and provide a downloader (the audio analogue of `img2dataset`) so anyone can reconstruct it; the Creative-Commons subset can be released as audio directly; and we can share full audio with research partners under appropriate agreements.

## 5. The Paralinguistic Inline-Annotation ASR Model

### 5.1 Why

A standard ASR model answers *what was said*. We need a model that answers *what was said **and how***, inline, with timestamps: where a vocal burst occurs and which type (giggle, shout, moan, sigh), and how the surrounding words are performed (whispered, shouted, sarcastic, trembling), including emotion, talking style, emphasis patterns, and temporal changes across a sentence. The annotation should be inspired directly by the EmoNet and VoiceNet taxonomies so that its labels compose with the rest of the stack.

### 5.2 Synthetic training data

We can construct supervised data with exact ground truth:

1. **Generate vocal bursts.** Using text-to-sound-effect models (e.g. MOSS-SoundEffect), synthesize ~16,000 vocal bursts spanning ~100 burst groups, drawn from the ~120-category vocal-burst taxonomy. Real bursts from existing datasets are mixed in where available.
2. **Concatenate into ASR streams.** Build sequences of the form *speech snippet → (sometimes) vocal burst → speech snippet → (sometimes) vocal burst → …*. Bursts appear only *sometimes* between snippets, so the model cannot learn to expect one at every boundary. Because we assemble the stream ourselves, we know the **speaker identity** and the **exact burst timestamps**.
3. **Bind bursts to the right speaker (multi-speaker case).** To teach speaker-attributed bursts, take a concatenated burst (say, laughter assigned to speaker 1) and **voice-convert** it to speaker 1's identity with a zero-shot VC model, so the burst genuinely sounds like that speaker. The model then learns to attribute bursts to the correct identity in multi-speaker audio.
4. **Enrich the labels.** Pair the stream with rich "how-it-was-said" descriptions produced by the emotion/captioning stack (Empathic Insight Voice Plus, BUD-E Whisper) and MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking — basic and fine-grained emotions, talking-style and vocal-burst descriptions.

This procedure extends trivially to more samples and more burst types.

### 5.3 Training and benchmarking

Take the best ASR base for which we have training code (candidates include Qwen3-ASR and the other in-house ASR base under evaluation), and fine-tune it — e.g. with a **LoRA** — to emit inline burst/emotion/style annotations. Benchmark with an **LLM-as-judge**: Gemini Pro is a strong judge, and **MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking** is an equally good judge that runs **locally**, which matters for cost and reproducibility. The Voice-Acting-Pipeline already uses Qwen3-ASR for word-level timestamps and Parakeet v3 for WER, so the scoring scaffolding is in place.

### 5.4 Deployment back onto the corpus

Once this model can annotate bursts, emotions, and general talking-style instructions, it becomes the annotator for **LAION Voice** (Section 6), upgrading the ~700k episodes already annotated with Empathic Insight Voice + Parakeet to inline, performance-aware transcripts.

## 6. LAION Voice — the In-the-Wild Corpus

### 6.1 Sources

- **~21M podcast episodes** from publicly downloadable links (mostly MP3/M4A), majority English with substantial multilingual coverage. ~700k are already annotated with Empathic Insight Voice + Parakeet; inline-ASR annotation will be strictly better.
- **~33M Creative-Commons-licensed YouTube audio tracks.**
- Additional non-CC podcasts/audiobooks/YouTube audio available for in-house annotation if desired.

### 6.2 Two-stage annotation (cost control)

Running a heavy annotator over tens of millions of files is wasteful when many contain little speech. So:

1. **Cheap first pass** — transcribe with a small, fast ASR (Parakeet, or even Whisper-base) to detect which files contain a meaningful fraction of speech.
2. **Heavy second pass** — run the paralinguistic inline-ASR model (and, where warranted, MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking) only on the speech-bearing files.

### 6.3 Quality filtering

Apply **DNS-MOS** for background-noise and speech-quality estimation, so every sample carries a quality score that downstream training can filter or weight on.

### 6.4 Release model

Following LAION-5B: for the non-permissive portion, release **source URLs + timestamps + annotations** plus an audio downloader (the `img2dataset` analogue) so anyone can reconstruct the audio locally; release the **CC subset as audio directly**; and share **full audio + transcriptions with research partners** under agreement.

## 7. LAION Voice Acting — the Synthetic Corpus

The predecessor here is **LAION's Got Talent** (built 2024–2025 by prompt-hacking GPT-4o audio into voice-acting scenes) — usable but not fully authentic. Modern open-weight voice-acting models are now good enough to generate large-scale, higher-quality data. **LAION Voice Acting** (a.k.a. *LAION's Got Talent v2*) is produced by two complementary pipelines that share a common post-processing and ranking stage.

A guiding principle across both: **always generate many candidates, never one.** Diminishing returns set in around ~25 candidates per group for DramaBox. Generating many candidates is what gives us magnitude variants and ranking headroom.

### 7.1 Pipeline 1 — Retrieval-Augmented Voice-Acting Generation

This pipeline exploits cloning models' ability to faithfully copy a reference clip's *style, emotion, and timbre* onto new text. The challenge is consent: cloning a real person's identity directly is not acceptable for release. The fix is a **two-hop identity laundering** step.

1. **Retrieve an emotion reference.** From the in-house emotion reference pool, retrieve a clip matching a target (age, gender, emotion) — optionally also annotated for language/accent/style via MOSS-Audio or BUD-E. This clip may be non-permissive (in-house only).
2. **Convert to a permissive identity.** Voice-convert the emotion reference onto a **permissive voice-identity target** — an Emilia-derived speaker cluster, or a synthetic identity from MOSS-VoiceGenerator / a voice-design model. The result keeps the *emotion* of the reference but the *identity* of a clearable speaker. **This converted clip becomes the actual reference.**
3. **Clone onto new text.** Feed the converted reference into a cloning TTS ([Echo TTS](https://huggingface.co/jordand/echo-tts-base) for English; MOSS-TTS v1.5 / VoxCPM2 / Fish-Speech v2 / Qwen3-TTS otherwise). Generate **several candidates** (5–20) varying temperature, seed, or speed.
4. **Repair.** Run every candidate through **RE-USE** to remove distortion and background noise.
5. **Audio super-resolution.** Run **LavaSR BWE** (bandwidth extension only, no denoising) to upsample the RE-USE output to 48 kHz with learned spectral detail. This adds high-frequency clarity without altering the natural vocal texture that RE-USE already cleaned.
6. **Rank and retain a spread.** Score candidates with a composite reward (Section 7.3) and keep the best — but **also** keep representative high / medium / low-quality samples, and samples ranked along a target emotion (anger, bitterness, gratitude, infatuation…). Those spreads become the instruction-tuning pairs of Section 7.5.

### 7.2 Pipeline 2 — Instruction-Based Voice-Acting Generation

Here the model itself understands voice-acting instructions — *how* to perform and *what* to say. Drivers: **DramaBox** (best English emotional fidelity — it covers the instruction-following scene-synthesis cases on its own), **VoxCPM2**, and **MOSS-VoiceGenerator**. The hard part is writing instructions diverse enough to cover the whole human range. We sample seeds from the taxonomies and expand them with an LLM (Gemma 4) into model-specific prompts.

**Prompt-seed sampling strategies (illustrative mix: 50% / 25% / 25%):**

- **(~50%) EmoNet + VoiceNet sampling.** Sample 1–3 EmoNet emotions, each with a magnitude (*slightly / clearly / extremely present*); always sample perceived age, perceived gender, and tempo; then sample additional VoiceNet dimensions. This yields prompts like "high physical tension, as if carrying a piano," "extreme ranting," "extreme whispering," or "preacher mid-sermon."
- **(~25%) Archetype + EmoNet/VoiceNet.** Draw one of ~920 literary/character archetypes (grouchy old captain, fantasy orc, squeaky fairy-tale mouse, cliché tech-bro) and combine with sampled emotions.
- **(~25%) Acting challenges.** Draw one of ~1,478 casting-style challenges (vulnerable speech to a parent with an emotional shift; deathbed farewell to a child in physical pain).

**Extended VoiceNet injection.** Into any of the three, occasionally inject situation-dependent dimensions — social context (theater stage vs. whispering in an elevator vs. office vs. relaxed home), body poses, and health/substance dimensions — including deliberately unusual or out-of-distribution combinations.

**Language note.** DramaBox is excellent for English and workable elsewhere with ranking, but mishandles ü/ö/ä — so the LLM must avoid those characters for DramaBox, and German umlaut content is routed to VoxCPM2 / MOSS-TTS v1.5 / Fish-Speech v2 instead. Non-English output should get native-speaker QA. However, DramaBox's emotional fidelity in non-English languages — even when the orthography is wrong — makes it a valuable *reference generator* for the hybrid strategy in Section 7.2.2.

### 7.2.1 Character-consistent prompting and scene splitting

#### Why same-identity emotion pairs matter

Future audio omni-models must be able to take a voice identity and perform it in any requested emotional register — angry, joyful, grief-stricken, whispering, ranting — while keeping the speaker recognizable. Training this capability requires paired data: *the same voice* performing the *same text or scene* with *different emotions and talking styles*. Such pairs are almost impossible to mine from wild audio, where speakers, recording conditions, and emotional contexts vary uncontrollably. Synthetic generation is the only scalable path.

The target supervision looks like this: given Scene 1 (a speaker performing with childlike joy) and an instruction ("now perform with intense pain"), the model should produce Scene 2 (the same voice, now in pain). Or: given angry ranting, produce vulnerable sadness. Given detached clinical analysis, produce trembling grief. The richer and more visceral the emotional contrast between the two scenes, the more useful the training pair — it forces the model to learn how to modulate emotion *independently* of identity.

Instruction-tuning pairs of the form *"here is a sentence performed with low bitterness — now perform it with extreme bitterness"* (magnitude pairs) and *"here is joy — now perform grief"* (polarity pairs) together cover the full landscape of emotion-conditioned voice control. Both require same-identity audio in different emotional states, which is exactly what character-consistent prompting produces.

#### Which models can do this

We tested three instruction-following TTS models for character-consistent generation:

- **VoxCPM2** — understands basic style instructions but cannot reliably execute a mid-clip emotional transition. When given a `CUT TO:` prompt, it either ignores the transition entirely (both halves sound the same), or breaks speaker identity across the boundary (Scene 2 sounds like a different person). Emotional contrast is inconsistent even when it does respond to the transition marker.
- **MOSS VoiceDesign / VoiceGenerator** — designs speaker timbres from free-text descriptions and can vary vocal style, but has no mechanism for controlled mid-generation emotional shifts. It produces a single consistent style per generation; the `CUT TO:` pattern is not part of its training or inference loop.
- **DramaBox (22B DiT)** — reliably honors `CUT TO:` emotional shifts while maintaining speaker identity. Because DramaBox is trained on theatrical performance data with stage directions, it natively understands the concept of "same actor, different emotional moment." The voice identity (age, gender, timbre, resonance) stays consistent while the emotional delivery, pace, volume, and talking style change dramatically across the `CUT TO:` boundary.

DramaBox is therefore currently the only known open-weights model that can produce character-consistent same-identity emotion pairs from text instructions alone. This makes the prompting template below a DramaBox-specific technique — though any future model that learns from this data could inherit the capability.

#### Prompting template

Every character-consistent prompt follows a rigid six-part structure. Each part is mandatory — omitting any element degrades either the speaker consistency or the emotional contrast.

**Part 1 — Recording quality frame.** The prompt opens by establishing that this is a *pristine, high-quality studio voice recording with no background noise*. This anchors DramaBox to produce clean, artifact-free audio without environmental sounds. Reinforce with *"genuine, spontaneous, realistically performed"* to push away from robotic or over-acted delivery.

**Part 2 — Speaker description.** Age, gender, timbre, voice quality — all in English. This description applies to *both* scenes and is the anchor for identity preservation. Example: *"A mature adult female voice, possessing a high soprano timbre with a bright, delicate harmonic texture and minimal low-end resonance."*

**Part 3 — Scene 1 emotional setup.** One to two sentences painting the emotional landscape *before* any dialogue. Describe the situation, the speaker's internal state, their pace and energy. Example: *"The actor is frozen mid-breath, overwhelmed by the sudden, profound weight of the declaration, struggling to reconcile the words with their own reality."*

**Part 4 — Scene 1 dialogue.** Double-quoted inline text (`"..."`) for spoken words, interleaved with stage directions in parentheses for vocal qualities, pauses, breaths, and delivery notes. Stage directions are never spoken — they guide the performance. Phonetic vocalizations like *"Hahaha"*, *"Mmm"*, *"Ugh"* can go inside quotes; sound effects like *sigh*, *gasp*, *cough* stay as stage directions.

**Part 5 — `CUT TO:` transition.** This is the structural pivot. The marker `CUT TO:` appears in capital letters, followed by one to three sentences describing *how* the emotional shift occurs and making explicit that this is *the same actor, the same voice, in a different scene with different emotions*.

The intensity of the transition description must match the intended contrast:

- **Abrupt, intense shifts** (surprise, sudden pain, explosive anger, overwhelming joy) — describe the transition in **emphatic language with strong verbs and exclamatory phrasing**: *"The adrenaline has VIOLENTLY drained away, leaving a hollow, shaky exhaustion!"* or *"SUDDENLY the same voice ERUPTS with raw, uncontrollable fury — the careful restraint is GONE!"* Capital letters and exclamation marks signal to DramaBox that the emotional change is extreme and immediate.
- **Subtle, gradual shifts** (gentle melancholy replacing contentment, quiet resignation after anger) — describe the transition in calm, measured lowercase prose: *"the careful hesitation gives way to a quieter, steadier tone — the same voice, now carrying the weight of reluctant acceptance rather than fresh grief."*

**Part 6 — Scene 2 performance.** New emotional context with stage directions and double-quoted dialogue, following the same interleaving pattern as Scene 1. Optionally close with a sentence about how the performance should feel across both moments.

```
Please, please, stop it right now!" (Voice tightens, syllables
rushed, higher register)

CUT TO:                                                                 ← [Part 5: Transition marker]

The adrenaline has violently drained away, leaving a hollow, shaky     ← [Part 5: Transition description — abrupt shift]
exhaustion; the tone drops significantly, becoming weak, strained,
and incredibly fragile as she attempts control.

(Breaths unevenly, voice wavering, sounding small and desperately      ← [Part 6: Scene 2 performance]
focused on the phone) "Hello? Yes, please... I... I think I touched
something wet. It hurt so much, I'm okay, I think, but I need
help, please."

The performance should maintain a fragile, immediate sense of          ← [Closing direction]
vulnerability through both moments.
```

Scene 1 is high-arousal panic (screaming, rushed syllables, elevated pitch). Scene 2 is low-arousal exhaustion (wavering, small, fragile). The speaker identity — a young girl with a high, bright timbre — stays constant. The `CUT TO:` transition explicitly describes how the adrenaline "violently drained away" and the tone "drops significantly," giving DramaBox clear instructions for the emotional pivot.

#### Emotion contrast design principles

The most valuable training pairs maximize the emotional distance between scenes:

| Contrast axis | Scene 1 → Scene 2 examples |
|---|---|
| **Polarity flip** | Joy → grief, affection → contempt, pride → shame |
| **Arousal shift** | Screaming rage → exhausted whisper, panic → numb calm |
| **Control shift** | Measured composure → emotional breakdown, ranting → quiet resignation |
| **Register shift** | Clinical detachment → raw vulnerability, formal authority → pleading desperation |

Within the prompt, always use language that ties the two scenes to the same person: *"the same actor"*, *"the same voice"*, *"the same speaker, now..."*, *"the voice shifts but remains recognizably..."*. This explicit identity anchoring is critical — without it, DramaBox may generate a subtly different speaker for Scene 2.

#### Post-processing: enhancement before splitting

Enhancement must happen on the **full continuous audio** (both scenes together), *before* splitting into Scene 1 and Scene 2. This ensures consistent audio quality and processing artifacts across both halves — if enhancement were applied after splitting, each scene could receive different noise profiles or spectral coloring, corrupting the identity match.

The enhancement chain is:

1. **RE-USE speech enhancement.** Removes residual TTS artifacts, mild distortion, and low-level noise. RE-USE operates in 15-second chunks with 1-second overlap and cross-fading, so the full character-consistent clip (~15–30 s typical) is processed seamlessly. RE-USE preserves most vocal bursts — gasps, sighs, laughter, breath sounds — because its SEMamba architecture is trained on speech that includes paralinguistic events. **Caveat:** RE-USE can degrade singing passages and background sound effects (music, environmental sounds). If the prompt deliberately includes singing or unusually intense vocal bursts (sustained screams, animal-like vocalizations), skip RE-USE and proceed directly to LavaSR BWE.
2. **LavaSR bandwidth extension.** Upsamples from 24 kHz to 48 kHz using Vocos-based super-resolution (enhance only, no denoising). This step is safe for all content types including singing and extreme vocalizations.

#### Audio splitting with Qwen3-ASR

After enhancement, the continuous audio is split into Scene 1 and Scene 2 at the `CUT TO:` boundary:

1. **Transcribe with word timestamps.** Qwen3-ASR (or Parakeet with forced alignment) transcribes the full audio, producing per-word start/end timestamps.
2. **Locate Scene 2 onset.** Parse the original DramaBox prompt to extract the first 2–3 words of Scene 2 dialogue (the first double-quoted text after `CUT TO:`). Match these words against the ASR transcript using word-stem matching (not exact string match — ASR may produce slight variations like "I" vs "i" or "it's" vs "its").
3. **Find the silence gap.** Locate the silence or near-silence region immediately *before* the matched Scene 2 words. This silence corresponds to the natural pause DramaBox inserts at the `CUT TO:` boundary.
4. **Split.** Cut the audio at the silence midpoint with 100 ms cross-fades on both sides. Scene 1 ends with a fade-out; Scene 2 begins with a fade-in.

The result is two aligned audio clips from the same speaker in different emotional registers, with ground-truth emotion labels derived directly from the prompt structure. These pairs feed into instruction-tuning as described in Section 7.5.

### 7.2.2 Hybrid strategy — Instruction-generated references for multilingual cloning

For English, emotionally rich reference audio is abundant (in-house pools, permissive datasets). For other languages — German, French, Spanish, Japanese, and the dozens of others we target — finding diverse, high-emotion reference audio with the right age, gender, accent, and emotional intensity is much harder. The hybrid strategy solves this by **using Pipeline 2's instruction-based generation to create emotional references for Pipeline 1's cloning workflow**.

**The insight:** DramaBox produces emotionally compelling audio in non-English languages, but with occasional spelling or pronunciation errors (especially with umlauts and diacritics). These errors make the audio unsuitable as a *final product*, but the *emotional performance* — the timbre, pacing, intensity, and affect — is often excellent. A voice-cloning model like **MOSS-TTS v1.5** can then copy that emotional style onto correctly spelled text, effectively separating emotional fidelity (from DramaBox) from textual accuracy (from the cloning model).

**Recipe:**

1. **Generate emotional references with DramaBox.** Use the taxonomy-sampled prompts from Pipeline 2, but instruct the LLM to write in the target language (German, French, etc.). DramaBox will produce audio with strong emotional delivery but potentially garbled orthography — misspelled umlauts, broken diacritics, occasional nonsense syllables. **This is acceptable** because this audio is used only as a *style reference*, never as a final output.
2. **Score and filter the references.** Rank the DramaBox candidates with Voice-CLAP (sanitized prompt, no WER gating — we *expect* WER to be high) and keep only the most emotionally expressive ones. The sanitized-prompt CLAP score selects for emotional match; WER is deliberately ignored at this stage because textual accuracy does not matter for a style reference.
3. **Clone onto correct text with MOSS-TTS v1.5.** Feed each selected DramaBox reference into MOSS-TTS v1.5 with the correctly spelled target text, the appropriate language tag (e.g. `language="German"`), and a **high temperature (1.8–1.9)** for diverse emotional variation. Generate ~10–20 candidates per reference. The high temperature encourages MOSS-TTS to explore the emotional space suggested by the reference rather than collapsing to a safe mean, producing a spread of intensity levels that feeds directly into the magnitude-pair supervision of Section 7.4.
4. **Post-process and rank.** The MOSS-TTS output goes through the standard RE-USE → LavaSR BWE → ranking pipeline (Section 7.3), this time *with* WER gating — the cloned output must now say the correct words.

**Why this works:** The two models complement each other at their weaknesses. DramaBox is emotionally unmatched but linguistically fragile outside English. MOSS-TTS v1.5 is linguistically robust across 31 languages and faithful at cloning emotional style, but needs a good emotional reference to clone *from* — and those references are exactly what DramaBox provides. The spelling errors in the DramaBox reference never propagate to the final output because MOSS-TTS generates from its own text input, not from the reference's words.

This hybrid path is especially valuable for **German** (where DramaBox's umlaut problems are worst but its emotional range is still best-in-class), and extends naturally to any language where high-quality emotional reference audio is scarce but emotional range is critical.

### 7.3 Shared post-processing and Best-of-N ranking

Both pipelines converge here. For an instruction-based group, the recipe is: generate ~25 candidates → **RE-USE** each (speech enhancement + denoising) → **LavaSR BWE** (bandwidth extension to 48 kHz, no denoising) → rank.

#### Voice-CLAP scoring models

**Voice-CLAP** is a contrastive language–audio model trained to score how well an audio clip matches a free-text description. We use two variants:

- **Voice-CLAP Large** (`laion/voiceclap-large`, 3584-dim embeddings via SentenceTransformer). Higher capacity, better discrimination of subtle emotional and stylistic nuance. Used as the **primary ranker**.
- **Voice-CLAP Small** (`laion/voiceclap-small`, 768-dim embeddings via AutoModel/AutoTokenizer). Faster, lower memory. Used as a cross-validation signal and for resource-constrained settings.

Both work the same way: encode an audio clip and a text description into a shared embedding space, then compute cosine similarity. Higher similarity = the audio better matches the text description.

#### Prompt sanitization

Raw DramaBox / VoxCPM2 prompts contain both stage directions (*"whispered, trembling with rage"*) and direct speech (*"I told you never to come back here"*). For ranking, we care about *how* the performance sounds, not *what words* it says — WER handles word accuracy separately. So we **sanitize** the prompt: strip all double-quoted dialogue via `re.sub(r'"[^"]*"', '', prompt)`, keeping only the emotional and directional context. This sanitized text becomes the **positive CLAP prompt**.

Example: a DramaBox prompt like *A woman in her 30s, whispering with barely contained fury: "I told you never to come back here." CUT TO: The same woman, now screaming: "Get out!"* sanitizes to *A woman in her 30s, whispering with barely contained fury:  CUT TO: The same woman, now screaming:* — the voice-quality description without the specific words.

#### Negative CLAP prompt

A fixed negative text — **"robotic, distorted, uncanny, distorted, distortion"** — is scored against every audio clip. Higher cosine similarity to this negative prompt means the audio sounds more synthetic or robotic (bad). **Subtracting** the negative similarity from the positive score penalizes synthetic-sounding candidates while rewarding natural, human-sounding output.

#### Ranking formulas

The primary ranking reward is:

**v_snr_L** = `(1 − WER) × (CLAP_sanitized_L − CLAP_neg_san_L + 2)`

where `CLAP_sanitized_L` is the Voice-CLAP Large cosine similarity to the sanitized prompt, `CLAP_neg_san_L` is the similarity to the negative prompt, and the `+2` offset keeps the multiplicative factor positive (cosine similarities are typically in [−1, 1], so the difference is in [−2, 2]; adding 2 maps this to [0, 4]). The `(1 − WER)` term ensures candidates that mangle the words are down-ranked regardless of how good they sound.

Additional ranking methods provide different views of quality:

| Method | Formula | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| **v_snr_L** (default) | `(1−WER) × (san_L − neg_L + 2)` | Primary: emotional fidelity minus roboticness, Voice-CLAP Large |
| **v_snr_S** | `(1−WER) × (san_S − neg_S + 2)` | Same logic with Voice-CLAP Small (cross-validation) |
| **v_san_L** | `(1−WER) × (san_L + 1)` | Positive prompt only, no negative subtraction (Large) |
| **v_san_S** | `(1−WER) × (san_S + 1)` | Positive prompt only (Small) |
| **content_enjoyment** | raw content_enjoyment score | Perceptual quality without prompt matching |
| **standard** | `(1−WER) × content_enjoyment` | Legacy: WER-gated perceptual quality |

The **v_snr_L** default works best in practice: the negative-prompt subtraction reliably demotes candidates that are technically on-prompt but sound robotic or distorted, which is the dominant failure mode of synthetic voice-acting TTS. The dual Large/Small cross-check catches cases where one model's embedding space has a blind spot.

Critically, **retain the whole ranked spread**, not just the winner. The middle and bottom of the distribution, and the per-emotion rankings, are the raw material for instruction-tuning pairs.

### 7.4 Single-GPU throughput benchmarks

All timings below are wall-clock measurements on a **single NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB** (CUDA 12, PyTorch 2.6, bfloat16), generating **25 candidates** from a representative 28-word dialogue prompt producing ~18.6 s of audio per sample. Models are loaded and unloaded sequentially (only one model in VRAM at a time). "First sample" includes any one-time CUDA warm-up or graph-tracing overhead; "warm" is the average of the subsequent samples.

#### TTS generation

| Model | Params | Load (s) | First (s) | Warm (s) | Compiled warm (s) | RTF | VRAM (GB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DramaBox** (DiT) | 22 B (4-bit) | 10.8 | 4.13 | 3.53 | 3.01 (−15%) | 0.16–0.19 | 16.3 |
| **MOSS-TTS v1.5** (AR) | 8 B | 9.6 | 8.14 | varies¹ | — | 0.40¹ | 24.2 |
| **Fish-Speech v2** (Qwen3, s2-pro) | 4 B | 50.9 | 53.9 | 49.3 | 6.46 (−87%) | 0.54–3.94 | 14.4–19.2 |

¹ MOSS-TTS v1.5 generates at a consistent ~33 tokens/s regardless of sequence position. Wall-clock time per sample ranges from 6–62 s because the autoregressive decoder produces variable-length token sequences at high temperature (1.85); short samples produce ~200 tokens in ~6 s (RTF ≈ 0.44), while 20% of samples hit the 2048-token cap at ~62 s producing ~161 s of audio (RTF ≈ 0.37). The overall RTF across 10 samples is 0.40 (2.5× faster than real time). There is no meaningful first-sample cold-start penalty; the variance is purely from output length, not from CUDA warm-up.

**DramaBox** dominates single-GPU throughput: its 30-step diffusion process runs at a fixed cost regardless of output length, giving a real-time factor of 0.16–0.19 (generating audio ~5–6× faster than real time). The first sample incurs a small CUDA warm-up overhead (~0.6 s), after which per-sample cost is stable at 3.53 s. With `torch.compile(mode="default", dynamic=True)` on the velocity model, warm throughput drops to 3.01 s/sample (−15%), but the first compiled sample takes ~230 s for graph tracing — a one-time cost that amortises over ~440 samples. DramaBox also supports batch generation via its `BatchSplitAdapter` (multiple samples per forward pass, VRAM-permitting). With 4-bit quantisation the 22 B model fits comfortably in ~16 GB.

**MOSS-TTS v1.5** is autoregressive over 32 VQ codebooks with a delay-pattern schedule. The Qwen3-based backbone supports SDPA, FlashAttention-2, and native batch generation (multiple prompts in a single `generate()` call, processing the batch in parallel). At temperature 1.85 the model explores diverse emotional variations but sometimes produces very long sequences. No `torch.compile` integration is currently available.

**Fish-Speech v2** (s2-pro checkpoint, Qwen3-based, 36-layer main transformer + 4-layer audio decoder with 10 codebooks) generates at ~5.5 tokens/s without compilation. With `torch.compile(backend="inductor", fullgraph=True)`, warm throughput improves **7.6×** — from 49.3 s to 6.46 s per sample (~12 s of audio each) — bringing its RTF from 3.94 down to 0.54. The first compiled sample takes ~220 s for graph tracing, amortising over ~40 samples. Audio quality and duration are comparable between compiled and uncompiled modes (12.0 s vs 12.6 s average output). Batching is not supported (the KV cache is fixed to `max_batch_size=1`).

#### Post-processing pipeline (per 25 candidates)

| Stage | Params | Load (s) | Total (s) | Per sample (s) | VRAM (GB) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RE-USE** (SEMamba) | 9.6 M | 5.4 | 21.3 | 0.85 | ~0 | CPU inference, sequential |
| **LavaSR BWE** | — | 0.4 | 1.3 | 0.05 | 0.1 | Vocos bandwidth extension |
| **Parakeet ASR** | 0.6 B | 13.8 | 0.9 | 0.03 | 2.6 | Batched (batch_size=25) |
| **Content enjoyment** | — | 0.7 | 1.1 | 0.04 | 1.0 | MLP head on audio features |
| **VoiceCLAP Large** | — | 9.0 | 2.7 | 0.11 | 18.9 | Cosine similarity scoring |
| **Ranking** | — | — | <0.01 | — | — | CPU arithmetic |
| **Post-processing total** | | **29.3** | **27.3** | | | |

Post-processing is cheap relative to generation: the full pipeline (RE-USE → LavaSR → ASR → CLAP → rank) takes ~27 s for 25 candidates, versus 88 s for DramaBox generation. Parakeet ASR benefits strongly from batching:

| Batch size | Total (s) | Per sample (s) | Speedup vs seq |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.22 | 0.219 | 1× |
| 5 | 0.25 | 0.051 | 4.3× |
| 10 | 0.37 | 0.037 | 5.9× |
| 25 | 0.81 | 0.032 | 6.8× |

Near-linear scaling up to batch_size=5, then diminishing returns as compute saturates. VoiceCLAP Large is the most VRAM-hungry scorer (18.9 GB for the 3584-dim SentenceTransformer) but processes each audio clip in ~0.1 s.

#### End-to-end DramaBox pipeline

For 25 candidates of ~18.6 s audio each:

| Phase | Time (s) | Share |
|---|---|---|
| TTS generation (DramaBox) | 88.4 | 76% |
| RE-USE speech enhancement | 21.3 | 18% |
| Scoring + ranking (LavaSR + ASR + CLAP) | 5.7 | 5% |
| Model loading (sum of all load times) | 40.1 | — |
| **Wall-clock total** | **115.7** | |

Generation is the bottleneck at 76% of total time. On an 8-GPU node, generation scales linearly (each GPU runs independent candidates), reducing the 25-sample generation from 88 s to ~11 s. Post-processing can be overlapped with generation on a separate GPU or batched after generation completes.

#### Scaling notes

- **Multi-GPU generation.** TTS generation is embarrassingly parallel: assign candidates round-robin across GPUs. An 8×A100 node generates 25 DramaBox candidates in ~11 s (wall clock), limited by the slowest GPU.
- **torch.compile.** Fish-Speech v2 benefits dramatically: `torch.compile(backend="inductor", fullgraph=True)` on the token decoder yields a **7.6× speedup** (49.3 → 6.46 s/sample), with ~220 s graph-tracing that amortises over ~40 samples. DramaBox's velocity model compiles to a 15% speedup (3.53 → 3.01 s/sample) at the cost of ~230 s, amortising over ~440 samples. Both compilation costs are one-time per process lifetime.
- **Batching.** DramaBox supports batch generation via `BatchSplitAdapter` (VRAM-permitting). MOSS-TTS v1.5 natively supports batch generation (multiple prompts in a single `generate()` call). Fish-Speech v2 is limited to single-sample generation (KV cache fixed to `max_batch_size=1`). For post-processing, Parakeet ASR achieves ~7× throughput improvement at batch_size=25 vs sequential (see table above). VoiceCLAP and content-enjoyment scorers process one audio at a time but are fast enough (~0.1 s/sample) that batching provides marginal benefit.
- **RE-USE bottleneck.** RE-USE runs on CPU and is the second-largest time cost. GPU-accelerated speech enhancement or parallel CPU workers could reduce this.

### 7.5 Why this data is unique

LAION Voice Acting yields supervision that cannot be mined from the wild:

- **Magnitude pairs.** Because we generate many candidates and rank them along an axis, we get the *same* utterance at low/medium/high quality, or at increasing anger / happiness / speed. → instruction-tuning pairs of the form *"here is a sentence performed with low bitterness — perform it with more bitterness."*
- **Same-speaker emotion contrasts with ground truth.** The character-consistent `CUT TO:` split gives the same identity in two known emotions → ground-truth targets for emotion-conditioned, voice-cloning-style instruction following.
- **Controllable breadth.** Taxonomy sampling lets us deliberately fill rare regions of emotion × style × situation space that the wild under-represents.

## 8. Outlook and Perspective

Put together, the program is a closed loop. The **taxonomies and scoring models** define and measure the space of human vocal expression. The **paralinguistic inline-ASR model** turns that capability into scalable annotation, converting **LAION Voice** from a pile of audio into performance-aware transcripts at the scale of tens of millions of episodes. **LAION Voice Acting** then fills the gaps the wild leaves open — emotional density, magnitude control, and same-speaker contrasts — entirely within permissive licensing.

The natural next step is to train the **audio omni-model** on the union of the two: LAION Voice for naturalistic breadth and grounding, LAION Voice Acting for controllability and instruction-following. The result we are aiming for is a model that can be *told*, in the vocabulary of EmoNet and VoiceNet, to whisper or rage or grieve — at a chosen intensity, in a chosen voice, with the right vocal bursts in the right places — and that can clone a reference identity into a new emotional register on command.

Concretely, the immediate work items are: (1) finalize and benchmark the paralinguistic inline-ASR model (synthetic data → LoRA → MOSS-Audio / Gemini judge); (2) run the two-stage annotation over LAION Voice and ship the URL-list + downloader release; (3) scale both voice-acting pipelines with the Best-of-N ranking and retain full ranked spreads for instruction tuning; and (4) begin omni-model training on the combined corpus.
